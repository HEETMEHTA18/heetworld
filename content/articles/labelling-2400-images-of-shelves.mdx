# What 2,400 labelled images of supermarket shelves taught me

When I started Retail Shelf Intelligence, I assumed the interesting work was the model. I was wrong. The interesting work — and 90% of the effort — was the data.

## The data is the model

Anyone can download a YOLO checkpoint. Few can say exactly what's on a specific shelf, in a specific store, under specific lighting. A shelf detection system is essentially a dataset with a wrapper around it.

The facts from my own project:

- **2,400+ images** collected and labelled by hand.
- **~40% of my time** went to preprocessing and relabelling, not training.
- Every accuracy gain I saw came from *fixing labels*, not from changing the architecture.

## What made it painful

**Glare.** Packaged goods are glossy. Reflections read as edges to a detector. I learned to treat glare as a data problem, not a model problem: more angles, better preprocessing, and honest labels for partially visible products.

**Class imbalance.** Three products accounted for most of the data and almost all of the easy wins. The long tail of niche SKUs is where the system actually failed — and where nobody wants to spend labelling effort.

**Boundary cases.** A product half-stocked looks "fine" to a detector and "empty" to a manager. If your labels can't distinguish these, your model won't either.

## The rule I'd teach anyone

> Label the boundaries, not the easy cases.

An hour spent labelling the confusing 5% of images moves accuracy more than five hours on the easy 95%. That's counterintuitive, because the easy 95% is where progress *feels* fastest.

## Why this matters beyond retail

Every ML project I've touched has the same shape: the model is the smallest, best-understood piece. The system — data quality, evaluation, deployment, monitoring — is where real engineering happens. Labelling those shelves was the best possible reminder of that.
